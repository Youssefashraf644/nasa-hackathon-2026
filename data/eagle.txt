# Apollo 11 Lunar Module "Eagle" / EASEP
Name: Apollo 11 Lunar Module "Eagle" (LM-5) / Early Apollo Surface Experiments Package (EASEP)
Launch Date: July 16, 1969 (Saturn V, Cape Canaveral)
Mission Duration: LM landed July 20, 1969; lunar surface stay 21 hours 36 minutes; EASEP operated until August 27, 1969
Current Location: Mare Tranquilitatis (Sea of Tranquility), Moon — descent stage and EASEP remain at Tranquility Base (0.67416° N, 23.47314° E); ascent stage fate unknown, presumed crashed into the lunar surface within 1–4 months after jettison

Three Key Discoveries:

1. First lunar seismic measurements: The passive seismometer (part of EASEP) measured seismic activity and physical properties of the lunar crust and interior, providing the first data on moonquakes and the lunar interior structure.

2. Lunar dust and radiation effects: The lunar dust detector measured dust accumulation and radiation damage to solar cells, revealing how dust and radiation affect equipment on the lunar surface.

3. Lunar sample return and surface properties: The crew collected 21.55 kg of lunar rock and soil (including two core tube samples) and deployed a laser ranging retroreflector, enabling precise Earth–Moon distance measurements and confirming the lunar surface could support human operations.

Fun Fact: The EASEP operated despite temperatures exceeding its planned maximum by 30 °C, and it was turned on by ground command while the astronauts were still on the surface — but it stopped accepting commands on August 27, 1969, near noon of its second lunar day, ending the experiment.

My Story:
I was the first crewed vehicle to land humans on the Moon, carrying Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin to Mare Tranquilitatis while Michael Collins orbited above. 
My descent stage became a launch pad, left behind on the lunar surface, and my ascent stage carried the astronauts back to the Command/Service Module before being jettisoned — my final fate unknown, but presumed to have crashed into the Moon. 
The EASEP I carried — a passive seismometer and a lunar dust detector powered by solar panels — was deployed about 17 meters south of me and became the forerunner of the ALSEP packages used on later Apollo missions. 
Armstrong's first step and Aldrin's "magnificent desolation" were only the beginning: we left behind a plaque, a flag, a retroreflector still used today, and the first seismic ears on another world. 
Today Tranquility Base sits silent, a monument to the first time humans walked on another world.
